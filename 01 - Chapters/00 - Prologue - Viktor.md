# Prologue

The ferry came in past the last headland a little before nine. Viktor Solberg stood at the rail with his coat buttoned wrong and let the town assemble itself out of the gray.

He had not expected it to be beautiful. That was the honest thing, the thing he would not say to anyone at the rådhus. He had read the file on Aursund as he read all files, for the numbers, and the numbers had told him a small, tired story: a fjord town at the end of a road that closed with snow, a population that had been falling politely for forty years, a budget held together with habit. He had built a picture to match, something drab and defensible, a place a man was sent when the party had decided he was more useful forgotten than employed. He had been ready for drab.

Instead the Aursfjord took the breath out of him. It came around the headland with none of the softness of a brochure: black water a thousand meters deep going straight up into black rock, the mountains dropping into it so steeply that the houses on the far shore looked pasted to the cliff, and over all of it a flat, shadowless sky the color of a coin. Pretty was too small a word for it. It was too large to be called just pretty, and it put him at precisely his own size, which was not large. For a long moment Viktor, who was seldom short of the next sentence, had nothing to say.

He had walked country like this once, years ago, on other fjords in a different life, before the committees, and he had never quite forgotten it. He had never set foot on this one. It did the same thing to him anyway, on his first morning of exile, and he had not come this far north expecting to be moved.

"You'll want to move along, then," the deckhand said, not unkindly, and Viktor realized the ramp was down and a man with a trolley of frozen goods was waiting on him to stop admiring the view.

"It's a remarkable fjord," Viktor said, because he could not quite help it.

The deckhand looked at the water as if checking that it was still there. "It's deep," he said. "Good for the freight. The cruise ones used to come in summer, before." He did not say before what. He hooked the trolley and went, and the fjord went back to being a road that happened to be made of water, which was, Viktor understood, how the people who lived here had always seen it.

He filed the exchange away without knowing he had, and went to find the town that owed him a career.

The rådhus was a square yellow building that had been added to at least three times by people who disagreed about roofs. Viktor's office was on the second floor. It had a view of the harbor, a desk two sizes too large for the room, and a smell of old paper that no amount of airing had defeated. He liked it immediately. A man could find things in a room like this.

By his second day he had found several.

He was, whatever else the party thought of him, extremely good at this. He read a budget as other men read the sports pages, with pleasure, even hunger, and Aursund's budget was the most interesting document he had encountered in years. Not because it was corrupt. He would almost have preferred corrupt. Corrupt he understood. This was something stranger. This was a town that spent real money, carefully, on things that made no sense at all, and wrote them down each year without apparent embarrassment, as though the making of sense were an optional feature the accounts had been ordered without.

There was, for example, a line for the annual inspection of a well. Viktor liked wells; a well was infrastructure. He asked to see it, and was informed that it could not be seen, because it had been filled in in 1964. The town nonetheless paid a man to inspect it every spring. Viktor asked what the man inspected, given that there was no well. The answer came back after some delay and some visible discomfort: that the man confirmed, each year, that the well was still filled in. For this the town paid him a modest sum, and had done so for sixty years without once concluding that the matter was settled.

There was a streetlight on Kirkegata, an ordinary sodium lamp, that the maintenance contract specifically forbade converting to the low-energy fittings that had replaced every other lamp in Aursund. When Viktor asked why, he was shown a bylaw. The bylaw did not say why either. It only said that the lamp on Kirkegata was to be maintained in its current form, and it had been signed, as far as anyone could tell, by a council that had otherwise left no trace of its deliberations.

There was a seat on the kommunestyre that was always kept empty. Not vacant in the sense of unfilled. Empty in the sense of reserved. Twenty-four seats and twenty-three members. A chair at the end of the table that no one sat in, that no one stood behind, and that appeared in the minutes, election after election, as present, abstained.

Viktor found all of this delightful, and said so, at the ten o'clock, to a room that did not find it delightful at all.

He had learned in his first week that here you removed things gently. The administration of Aursund was a small, watchful organism, and it had survived a great many clever men from the capital by the simple method of agreeing with everything and doing nothing. So he did not announce a purge. He praised their diligence. He talked about efficiency in the tone other men used for religion, warm and reasonable, and he proposed, only proposed, that they begin by looking together at the small things, the harmless things, the lines that surely everyone agreed were relics.

They agreed with him about the well, the lamp, and the empty chair. It was the kind of agreement you give a man about the weather at a funeral, flat, complete, and meaning nothing. And then they found reasons, one after another, gentle reasons, why each specific thing might be left exactly as it was for now.

The registrar was the worst of them, or the best, depending. She was a heavy, careful woman named Ingrid who had worked in the building longer than Viktor had been in politics. When he pressed on the empty chair, she looked at him for a long moment and said that it had always been like that.

"Everything has always been like that," Viktor said, pleasantly. "That's what a town is. Then somebody arrives and asks why, and it turns out half of it can simply stop."

"Some of it can," Ingrid agreed. She squared a stack of paper that was already square. "You'll learn which half."

He took that for the ordinary resistance of the ordinary clerk, which is what it looked like, and he was not entirely wrong. He was only not entirely right, and the difference between the two would take him the better part of a winter to find.

Bus Line 17 was not on his list of relics. It came to the list on its own, which pleased him, because the best cuts were the ones a budget made for you.

The line ran from the harbor up the valley to Storfjellet and back, six times a day, and five of those runs were reasonable. People used them. Children rode the 07:10 to school and came home on the 15:20. The midday runs carried the old and the carless to their appointments. Viktor had no quarrel with any of it. He was not a man who cut buses for the sport of it. A bus was a town keeping its promises to the people who could not drive, and he approved of promises that could be costed.

It was the last run he could not make sense of. The 23:40. Every night, seven nights a week, a driver took an empty bus from the harbor up the dark valley to the turning circle below Storfjellet, waited the regulation four minutes, and brought it back. The town paid for the fuel, the driver's hours, and the wear on a vehicle that carried, according to the ridership figures Viktor requested and reread twice, an average of zero passengers. Not almost zero. He had been ready to be told almost, and to make an allowance for the one old man who could not sleep. The figure was zero. The 23:40 had, in the memory of the recording system, never once carried a fare.

He asked, of course. He was good at asking. He asked the transport office. The transport office said the last run had always been made. He heard the word always land again in that flat local register, the register he was beginning to think of as the sound a town made when it had stopped remembering the reason for something and kept doing it out of a fear it could not name. He asked how long always was. It took two days and a trip into the archive to answer. The answer, when it came, had a weight to it that Viktor registered as satisfaction and nothing else.

The 23:40 had run every night for a hundred and nineteen years. There was no record of a night it had been missed. Not in the war. Not in the winter of '66, when the road was shut for five weeks and the ferry with it. Somewhere in a drawer there was a note, in a hand no one living could identify, to the effect that the last service was on no account to be discontinued. The note gave no reason. Viktor had by now read a great many such notes. He had developed a professional contempt for the men who wrote them, men who mistook their own forgetfulness for wisdom and handed the bill to everyone who came after.

He put the cut in the autumn budget with the well, the lamp, and the reserved chair. Unlike those three, it went through without a single gentle reason raised against it. The council passed the autumn measures on the twentieth, in an afternoon session that ran short, and the empty seat abstained along with the rest.

Ingrid brought him the signed resolution herself. She set it on the enormous desk and stood a moment longer than the errand required.

"So that's the seventeen, then," she said.

"The last run," Viktor said. "The others stay. Nobody loses their bus." He was mild about it. He had won; there was no need to be otherwise. Nor did he want the old registrar to think him careless of the town. "It carried no one, Ingrid. For a hundred years it carried no one. Whatever it was for, the town it was for is gone."

"I expect so," she said.

She did not argue. He noticed that she did not argue, and he noticed that he had expected her to. He set both observations aside as the tiredness of a long day. The timetable change would take effect at the end of the month. On the last Sunday in September the 23:40 would run for the final time, and on the Monday the schedule would show, for the first time in a hundred and nineteen years, a gap where the last service had been. Viktor thought of it, when he thought of it at all, as a small clean line drawn through a small dead thing, and he was proud of it as he was proud of every waste he had ever ended.

That evening he walked down to the harbor, because the office had begun to smell of him as well as of paper. He stood where the ferry came in and watched the fjord go dark. This far north it went slowly, without drama, the light thinning out of the sky over the better part of an hour instead of falling. The mountains held the last of it long after the water had gone black, dark shapes against a sky still pale with a day that would not quite end, and the whole enormous still thing of it stopped him where he stood. A gull called once, and then nothing. He was not a sentimental man; he distrusted the word. But he understood, on the empty quay, why somebody might build a life around water like this and call it reason enough. He understood it against his own better judgment, which was the only kind of understanding he had ever fully trusted. Behind him the town settled into its evening, lamps coming on in the low houses, one on Kirkegata burning its old expensive yellow among all the tidy white ones. Viktor Solberg stood in the cooling dark, and for the first time since they had sent him here, he was almost content.

He had found the thing that was wrong, and he had begun to put it right. There was a great deal more of it, he could tell. It would keep him busy through the winter, and busy was close enough to happy for a man in his position.

Up the valley, in the dark below Storfjellet, a bus he had never seen turned in an empty circle and started back down toward the harbor, as it had every night for a hundred and nineteen years.
