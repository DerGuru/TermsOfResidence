# One

The track up to the house had not been graded since she was a girl, and the rental car took it stone by stone while Nora read the building as she read any she had not been paid to save. The roof was sound, more or less. The gutters were a season past clearing. Someone had repointed the north gable in the wrong mortar, a hard gray cement that would hold ten years and then tear the stone apart when it finally let go. Whoever had done it was long past being told so.

She had driven up through a town that looked as though it had been told it was dying and had decided, out of stubbornness, to take its time about it. The chandler was shut. The quay had two boats where she remembered ten. A hand-lettered sign in the co-op window advertised a meeting about the ferry timetable, the paper gone soft with damp. It was the kind of place you left. She had proof. She had left it.

The house carried her family's name. Or the family carried the house's; she had never been sure which way it ran. Aalvik. It was on the old survey and in her passport. It was on the letter folded in her coat too, the one that had brought her the length of the country to sign three papers and be gone. She had told herself it would take a weekend.

The kitchen light was on before she cut the engine. Her mother had the door open before she reached it, as she always had, as though she had been at the window adding up the road. Marit was smaller than the last time. Upright, on purpose, a posture held against something she never named.

"You didn't say which ferry," Marit said.

"I drove the last stretch."

"You'll be hungry."

It was not a question, so Nora let it stand. She came into the warmth and set her bag by the door. The house closed around her with its smell of wax, cold stone, and old smoke, the smell that had lived under thirty years of other people's cooking. It had not changed. She had been counting on it changing.

Her mother put out bread, cured lamb, and brown cheese. Nora ate more than she wanted, because eating was easier than talking. The clock over the door was the same clock. The chair with the mended leg was the same chair, mended the same clumsy way, which meant Tom had never come back to do it properly as he had promised twenty years ago.

Her father had lasted in this kitchen until Nora was nine. He had wanted, in the end, only ordinary things: a holiday that did not turn for home at the first sign of dark, a wife who would once, just once, come with him and stay the night somewhere else. He had not got them. Nobody in this house got them. Eventually he stopped asking, then simply stopped, like a tenant leaving, taking his coat and none of the furniture.

"You've let the gutters go," Nora said.

"I've let a lot of things go." Marit said it without weight, cutting bread. "You'd know."

Nora let that stand too. In her own head she had come home four days early, rehearsing this, and none of the rehearsals had survived the first ten minutes. They never did.

She waited until the plates were cleared to put the folder on the table. She had a restorer's respect for paper; you did not wave it about, you laid it down and let it speak for itself. "I talked to a lawyer in Bergen," she said. "About the house. About settling it, so it isn't hanging over everything."

Her mother wiped a spot on the counter that was already clean.

"You keep the right to live here," Nora said. "That doesn't change. Nothing changes for you. We put a name on it that the world recognizes, and if something happens then, it's simple, not like now." She turned the top page to face her mother. "Right now it isn't simple. Right now it's the strangest title I have ever seen, and I have seen churches."

That was true, and it was the part that had kept her awake in the hotel outside Trondheim. She had read a great many deeds. Old buildings lied all the time, a corridor added here, a wall knocked through there, a drawing that quietly stopped matching the thing it stood for. You expected mess. What you did not expect was the Aalvik file, which refused to resolve at all.

The land belonged to the kommune. The building belonged to a foundation whose papers named no living officer and gave no purpose beyond its own continuation. Her family held neither. Her family held a right of use, worded in a hand nobody had signed, that passed from mother to daughter and could not be sold, because it was not a thing you could hand to a stranger. There was no owner to buy out. There was no owner at all, only three parties leaning on one another like a house of cards, and any paper she filed to make it clean would need the signature of someone who did not exist.

There was one more line near the bottom, in the same unsigned hand: a condition on the right of use. Its holder was to spend every night in the house. Nora had read covenants that fixed the color of a roof and the height of a fence. She had never read one that fixed where a grown woman slept.

"It can't be sold," Marit said. Not a question either.

"Not as it is. That's what I want to fix." Nora kept her voice level, the voice she used with clients who loved a ruin more than they could afford to. "There's a man at the kommune now, the new one. He's going through exactly these cases, clearing them up. For once the town wants what I want. I filed the request on the way north. It's on someone's desk already."

Something crossed her mother's face and was gone before Nora could name it. Asked, she would have called it fear, and she would have filed it under the same heading as everything else her mother did, which was: making herself the one thing that could not be moved.

"Withdraw it," Marit said.

"Withdraw what?"

"The request. Whatever you filed." Marit sat down across from her, which she did not often do; she was a woman who stayed on her feet in her own kitchen. "Leave it as it is."

"Leave it." Nora heard her own voice go flat. "Mamma, you can't leave it. That's the whole problem. It's been left a hundred years. Now it's mine to leave, and I don't want it. I want to not think about this house. Can you understand that? I want to sign one thing and never think about it again."

"I understand." Her mother's hands lay flat on the table. "But I'm asking you not to."

They sat with that. Out the window the light was going off the water, the long slow northern leaving that took an hour to finish. Nora made herself breathe out.

"Then come with me," she said. "File it, don't file it, whatever you want. But you can't manage the winters alone anymore, we both know that. Come south. Stay with me while we work it out. A week. You haven't left this valley in my whole life."

"I can't."

"You can. That's the thing, you can. There's nothing here a neighbor couldn't see to for a week."

"I can't leave the house at night." Her mother said it as she had said it all through Nora's childhood, flat and complete, a sentence with no door in it. Not I don't want to. Not it's hard for me. I can't. That sentence had cost Nora a father, or near enough. It had cost every school play her mother never came to, every outing that ended at dusk, every time a small Nora had understood that the house came first and she came second.

"It's the condition on the right." Marit nodded at the folder as though it might argue. "Somebody sleeps in the house every night, or the right lapses. That's how I've kept the roof over me. I never had the money for another, so I kept this one." She smoothed the cloth on the table. "And you get so you don't like the thought of the dark finding it empty. But it's the condition. That's all it needs to be."

That last part she added quickly, like closing a drawer she hadn't meant to open.

It was a good reason. It was true. Nora believed all of it and none of it at once. There had been years when a determined woman could have broken a lease, taken the loss, and gone. Her mother had stayed like water in a low field, not held, only lower than everything around it. Ask her why, and she would show you the clause. Nora had long since stopped believing the clause was all there was to it.

"Right," Nora said. "Of course. The house."

"It isn't—"

"It's always the house." She was too tired to be angry properly, which was worse; the anger came thin and old. "Fine. Then I'll do it. Tonight. You take my car, you drive to Solveig's, you sleep one night in forty years in a bed that isn't in this house, and I'll stay here. I'll sleep here. Whatever it is you think you're holding up, I'll hold it up for one night. Let me do one thing for you."

She had meant it as a dare more than an offer. She expected her mother to wave it off, to say don't be silly, to make it about Nora not knowing the trick of the boiler. That was why it caught her completely off guard when her mother went very still.

"No."

"No?"

"Not you." Marit's voice had changed. Quieter, and under the quiet something Nora had no name for. "Anyone else. Not you."

"That doesn't even make sense."

"You asked me to trust you with the paper." Her mother stood, gathering plates that were already gathered. "Trust me with this. Not you, not in this house at night. Promise me that."

And there it was again, the old machine, running as it had always run. Her mother would sooner sleep every night of her life in this cold house than take one night of help from her daughter, and would dress it up as protection, because that was the shape the martyrdom took when it had nowhere else to go. Nora had come home for a weekend to sign three papers. She could see already that she would not get to sign anything, that the house would do to this visit what it did to everything, which was hold on.

"I'm staying till it's sorted," Nora said. "Here. I'm too old to live out of a suitcase, and there's no sense paying for a hotel when there's a bed in this house. Not because I'm holding anything up. Only because it's practical."

Her mother looked at her a long moment. Whatever she wanted to say, she put it back where she kept things like that.

"Take the small room," Marit said. "Not the cellar. The cellar door stays shut."

"I wasn't going to sleep in the cellar."

"I'm just telling you that it stays shut." She said it lightly now, almost ordinary, hanging the cloth over the tap. "The lock is good. Leave it locked. Your grandmother put that lock on, and it's better than the door it hangs on, so leave it be."

Nora had noticed the lock on her way in. A heavy old thing, far too serious for a cellar door, newer than the wood by fifty years, and kept up as nothing else in the house. She had thought: someone loved that lock. She had thought nothing else about it, because it was a locked door in an old house, and old houses were full of them.

Out on the water the ferry sounded, the last one down the fjord, and it held the note longer than a ferry needs to, longer than Nora remembered any ferry holding it. It was a flat brass sound that went out across the town and came back off Storfjellet a beat late. Her mother stopped with the cloth in her hand and listened for the second sound after the first.

"You remember where the extra blankets are." It was not a question. Nothing she said ever quite was.
